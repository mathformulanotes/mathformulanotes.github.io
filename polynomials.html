Polynomials

A polynomial is a finite sum of terms $a_k x^k$. The notes below cover how to read its degree, how to add and multiply two polynomials, and how division reveals factors.

  • Degree and leading coefficient
  • Adding and multiplying
  • Division and the factor theorem

Degree and leading coefficient

The degree of a nonzero polynomial is the largest exponent with a nonzero coefficient. That coefficient is the leading coefficient. A constant polynomial such as $7$ has degree $0$; the zero polynomial is usually said to have undefined degree. A math solver can expand a messy product before you read off the degree.

Worked example

$f(x)=4x^3-x+9$ has degree $3$ and leading coefficient $4$. The polynomial $6-2x^5+x^2$ has degree $5$ and leading coefficient $-2$, even though the terms were not written in standard order.

Adding and multiplying

Add or subtract by combining like powers of $x$. To multiply, distribute every term of one factor across the other and then combine like terms. For two binomials the familiar FOIL pattern is just that distribution:

$$(x+p)(x+q) = x^2 + (p+q)x + pq.$$
Worked example

Expand $(x+3)(x-1)$: $x^2 + (-1+3)x + (3)(-1) = x^2 + 2x - 3$. For a slightly longer product, $(2x-1)(x^2+4)=2x^3+8x-x^2-4=2x^3-x^2+8x-4$.

Division and the factor theorem

If a polynomial $f(x)$ is divided by the monic linear factor $x-c$, the remainder is the number $f(c)$. In particular, $x-c$ is a factor of $f(x)$ if and only if $f(c)=0$. Completing the square is a different rewriting, useful when a quadratic does not factor over the integers; a math solver can show that form beside the factored form.

Worked example

Divide $f(x)=x^3-2x^2-5x+6$ by $x-1$ with synthetic division, using $c=1$:

$$ \begin{array}{r|rrrr} 1 & 1 & -2 & -5 & 6 \\ & & 1 & -1 & -6 \\ \hline & 1 & -1 & -6 & 0 \end{array} $$

The remainder is $0$, so $x-1$ is a factor, and $f(x)=(x-1)(x^2-x-6)=(x-1)(x-3)(x+2)$. That agrees with the direct checks $f(1)=0$, $f(3)=0$, and $f(-2)=0$.
