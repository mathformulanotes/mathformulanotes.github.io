Geometry and trigonometry formulas

These identities cover right triangles, coordinate geometry, common area and volume formulas, and the two main laws for general triangles.

Pythagorean theorem

In a right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$,

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2.$$

The same relation finds a missing leg: $a = \sqrt{c^2 - b^2}$. A math solver can check the arithmetic when the side lengths are not obvious integers.

Worked example

A ladder is $17$ feet long and its base sits $8$ feet from a wall. How high does it reach, if the ground and wall meet at a right angle?

  1. The height $h$ satisfies $8^2 + h^2 = 17^2$.
  2. $h^2 = 289 - 64 = 225$.
  3. $h = 15$ feet. The sides $8$, $15$, and $17$ form a known Pythagorean triple, which confirms the result.

Distance and midpoint

On the coordinate plane, the distance between $A(x_1,y_1)$ and $B(x_2,y_2)$ is the length of the hypotenuse of the right triangle with legs $|x_2-x_1|$ and $|y_2-y_1|$:

$$d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$$

The midpoint of segment $AB$ is the average of the coordinates:

$$M = \left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$$

To run the same steps on another pair of points, a math solver for the distance formula is a quick check.

Worked example

Find the distance and midpoint between $(1,2)$ and $(4,6)$.

  1. $d = \sqrt{(4-1)^2 + (6-2)^2} = \sqrt{9+16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
  2. $M = \left(\dfrac{1+4}{2},\ \dfrac{2+6}{2}\right) = (2.5,\ 4)$.

Area and volume formulas

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Triangle area} &= \tfrac{1}{2}bh \\ \text{Circle area} &= \pi r^2 \\ \text{Cylinder volume} &= \pi r^2 h \\ \text{Sphere volume} &= \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \end{aligned}$$

Keep units consistent: if $r$ and $h$ are in centimeters, volume is in cubic centimeters. Leave answers in terms of $\pi$ unless a decimal approximation is requested.

Worked example

A cylinder has radius $3$ cm and height $10$ cm. Its volume is $\pi(3)^2(10) = 90\pi$ cubic centimeters, or about $282.7$ cm$^3$ if you use $\pi \approx 3.1416$.

SOHCAHTOA

In a right triangle, if $\theta$ is an acute angle,

$$\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\quad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\quad \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}.$$

The mnemonic SOH-CAH-TOA is just a memory aid for those three ratios. More identity lists live on the math solver trigonometry pages.

Worked example

A right triangle has opposite side $5$, adjacent side $12$, and hypotenuse $13$ (since $5^2+12^2=13^2$). Then

$$\sin\theta = \frac{5}{13},\quad \cos\theta = \frac{12}{13},\quad \tan\theta = \frac{5}{12}.$$

Law of sines and law of cosines

For any triangle with sides $a$, $b$, $c$ opposite angles $A$, $B$, $C$,

$$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}$$

The law of cosines generalizes the Pythagorean theorem to triangles that may not be right-angled:

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$$

Use the law of sines when you know a side and its opposite angle (ASA, AAS, or a well-behaved SSA case). Use the law of cosines for SAS or SSS.

Worked example

In triangle $ABC$, $a = 7$, $b = 10$, and $C = 60^\circ$. Find side $c$.

  1. $\cos 60^\circ = \dfrac{1}{2}$, so $c^2 = 7^2 + 10^2 - 2\cdot 7\cdot 10\cdot \dfrac{1}{2}$.
  2. $c^2 = 49 + 100 - 70 = 79$.
  3. $c = \sqrt{79}$. (That is about $8.89$ if a decimal is needed.)
