Linear equations

A linear equation in one variable can be rearranged so that all terms with $x$ are on one side and all constant terms are on the other. Adding the same number to both sides, or multiplying both sides by the same nonzero number, keeps the equation balanced.

Worked example

Solve $3x - 7 = 2x + 5$.

  1. Subtract $2x$ from both sides: $x - 7 = 5$.
  2. Add $7$ to both sides: $x = 12$.
  3. Check: $3(12) - 7 = 29$ and $2(12) + 5 = 29$. Both sides match.

Quadratic formula and discriminant

Any quadratic equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a \neq 0$ has solutions

$$x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$

The expression under the square root is the discriminant, $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$. If $\Delta > 0$, there are two distinct real roots. If $\Delta = 0$, there is one real root (a repeated root). If $\Delta < 0$, there are no real roots; the two solutions are complex conjugates. A math solver is handy when you want to confirm $a$, $b$, $c$ and the two resulting roots together.

Worked example

Solve $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$. Here $a = 1$, $b = -5$, and $c = 6$.

  1. $\Delta = (-5)^2 - 4(1)(6) = 25 - 24 = 1$. Two real roots.
  2. $x = \dfrac{5 \pm 1}{2}$.
  3. So $x = 3$ or $x = 2$. In factored form the equation is $(x-3)(x-2) = 0$, which agrees.

Factoring patterns

Recognizing a pattern often shortens the work. The most common identities are:

$$\begin{aligned} a^2 - b^2 &= (a-b)(a+b) \\ a^2 + 2ab + b^2 &= (a+b)^2 \\ a^2 - 2ab + b^2 &= (a-b)^2 \end{aligned}$$

For a monic trinomial $x^2 + sx + p$, look for two numbers that add to $s$ and multiply to $p$. If a leading coefficient other than $1$ is in the way, try grouping or check the work with a math solver after you have a candidate factorization.

Worked example

Factor $x^2 + 5x + 6$. The pair $2$ and $3$ adds to $5$ and multiplies to $6$, so

$$x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x+2)(x+3).$$

Expanding the right-hand side gives $x^2 + 5x + 6$, which matches. As a second check, $9^2 - 4^2 = 81 - 16 = 65$ and $(9-4)(9+4) = 5 \cdot 13 = 65$.

Exponent rules

For a nonzero base $a$ and integers $m$ and $n$:

$$\begin{aligned} a^m \cdot a^n &= a^{m+n} \\ \frac{a^m}{a^n} &= a^{m-n} \\ (a^m)^n &= a^{mn} \\ a^0 &= 1 \\ a^{-n} &= \frac{1}{a^n} \end{aligned}$$

The same product and quotient rules hold for rational exponents once a principal root is defined. Use a math solver if a messy mix of negative and fractional powers needs a simplified form.

Worked example

Simplify $\dfrac{2^5 \cdot 2^{-1}}{2^2}$.

  1. Combine the numerator: $2^5 \cdot 2^{-1} = 2^{4}$.
  2. Divide: $2^{4}/2^{2} = 2^{2} = 4$.
  3. In one line: $2^{5-1-2} = 2^{2} = 4$.
